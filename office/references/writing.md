# Write reports and plans for their intended use

Use for new or substantially rewritten prose, including narrative in a workbook.
Follow the requested genre, audience, language and house style. Preserve required
template headings and source field names. For limited edits, review the changed
passages and their necessary context rather than rewriting the document.

## Organize the document around the reader's task

Establish what the recipient needs to understand, decide or carry out. Use that
purpose to select and order the content. The opening should state the subject,
scope and main finding or recommendation, including any condition essential to
interpreting it. Introduce background when the reader needs it to follow the
argument, rather than making the reader reconstruct the conclusion from it.

Choose a structure suited to the work product. A performance review explains
material changes and their implications; a plan connects proposed activities to
resources, responsibilities and timing; a research synthesis organizes findings
around agreements, differences and their significance. These are purposes to
serve, not mandatory section templates.

Keep the reasoning needed to assess the main conclusion in the main narrative.
Place detailed derivations or supporting records in notes or an appendix when
they remain useful but interrupt that narrative. Combine sections that repeat
the same point; separate sections only when they serve distinct reader needs.

## Use headings to make the structure clear

The document title should identify its subject and purpose. For formal reports,
use concise, descriptive section headings with established domain terminology.
Name the object and aspect being examined, such as a cost comparison, funding
requirement or implementation schedule. A heading should tell the reader what
the section contains without relying on the preceding conversation.

Keep headings at the same level comparable in scope and grammatical form.
Subheadings should divide the parent topic into meaningful parts. When a brief
benefits from a finding-led heading, state the supported finding with its
material qualification; do not promote a conditional estimate into a promise.

Internal working questions and review instructions belong in the analysis
process. Rewrite them for the document's purpose instead of carrying over
conversational prompts, coaching language, slogans or metaphors. Preserve a
question-based structure when it serves the requested genre, such as a FAQ,
interview, lesson or explicitly framed research question.

## Explain the findings in connected prose

Develop each paragraph around a substantive point. Connect the relevant evidence
to the interpretation and explain its consequence for the reader. State how a
comparison supports the recommendation rather than presenting numbers followed
by an unexplained choice. Use tables for comparable values, options or schedules;
use prose to explain relationships that a table alone leaves implicit.

Use wording that distinguishes reported facts, analytical judgments and proposed
actions. Place assumptions and limitations beside the conclusions they affect,
and explain how they change the recommendation, confidence or next action.
Consolidate recurring qualifications instead of repeating a general disclaimer
in every section.

Prefer concrete subjects, precise verbs and familiar terms. Match technical
detail to the recipient's knowledge and decision. Formality should come from
accuracy and disciplined organization, not inflated vocabulary, abstract noun
phrases or an account of the agent's drafting and validation steps.

## Review the completed text

Read the title and heading outline together, then the opening and each section's
main point. Check that they describe the actual content and build toward the
document's purpose. Repair missing reasoning, unsupported emphasis and shifts in
terminology. Remove repetition or background that does not help the reader assess
or use the result. Recheck the saved output after edits so that headings, prose,
tables and cross-references still agree.
