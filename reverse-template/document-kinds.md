# What kind of document is this?

Both document branches ask this before anything else, and they answer it here so
that one document does not get a different package for having arrived as a
`.docx` rather than a `.pdf`. The branch decides how to package; this decides
what the package promises.

Placeholder text does not decide it. A contract, an invitation and a set of
minutes all carry placeholders — `_______`, `[insert here]`, `Facilitator name`.
The question is not which words are obviously replaceable. It is **what a new
one has to keep**, and each kind answers that differently.

| Kind | A new one keeps | Its text may |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form | every word outside the blanks, verbatim | fill a blank, nothing else |
| Card | the artwork and where each line sits | barely move — shorten the wording, never the type or the spacing |
| Record | the section headings | grow and shrink block by block, by repeating or dropping a whole block |
| Article | the styles | run to any length; sections come and go |

Read the rendered pages and take the first match.

## 1. Form — blanks set into text that otherwise stands word for word

A contract, an application, a policy acceptance, an official return. Its pages
leave blanks — a run of underscores, a rule drawn under spaces, a parenthesised
`(NAME)`, an initial line — and around them sit clauses, terms and recitals that
a new one reproduces exactly. Filling it changes who and how much; it does not
change what the document says.

The test is the text around the blanks, not the blanks. Both parts have to hold:
there are blanks, **and** most of the page is text whose wording carries
obligation or meaning that a new instance must not restate in its own words.

A card's `[insert here]` fails the second part — there is no body of fixed
wording, only a few lines that are all replaceable. Check that before calling
something a form.

## 2. Card — artwork carries the page and the text is a handful of short lines

An invitation, a certificate, a notice, a cover.

The design is the image and the placement, neither of which a description
reproduces. There are no sections; there are a few short lines set where the
artwork leaves room. A new one says the same few things about a different date
or name, and a longer line breaks the composition instead of reflowing.

A letterhead logo does not make a form or a record a card. The artwork has to be
carrying the page, with the text down to a handful of lines.

## 3. Record — the same section headings come back in every instance

Minutes, a status report, a case sheet, a résumé, a checklist.

Its headings are slots rather than subject matter: `Roll call`,
`Unfinished business`, `Adjournment` appear in next month's minutes too. Under
them everything is replaced, and at least one list is a different length every
time — attendees, issues, action items, jobs held.

Ask what the *next* instance is called. If it keeps these headings, it is a
record; if it picks new ones, it is an article.

## 4. Article — anything else

A report, a manual, a policy, a whitepaper. Written top to bottom, its sections
are its subject rather than its shape, and the next one is another subject at
another length.

Only here is the reusable asset a style sheet. The other three ship the file,
because their value is in the arrangement, the artwork or the wording, and a
style sheet carries none of those.

## Where this is easy to get wrong

- **A contract reads like an article.** Two dozen numbered sections, a heading
  hierarchy, no images. The blanks are what separate them, so ask question 1
  first.
- **A résumé is not an article.** Its sections come back in every résumé, and
  the experience list is a different length each time. That is a record.
- **A whitepaper and a set of minutes both carry headings.** Only the minutes
  keep theirs next time.
- **Sparse pages are not slides.** A card can hold less text than a slide and is
  still a document; the dispatcher's rule about paper sizes applies here too.
- **Getting a form wrong is the expensive one.** Calling a contract a record
  invites a new instance to rewrite an indemnity clause in its own words. The
  other three mistakes cost a re-run.
